## Product intent

Lovely System Teambuilder will develop a cooperative shooting gallery application.

The intended application allows one to three robot lions to engage moving objects in a shared field. The mature concept may include:

- a common targeting reticle or sphere;
- objects following visible arcs through or around that common target area;
- normal objects requiring no more hits than the number of available shooters;
- abnormal objects that are harder to destroy outside the reticle and easier to destroy within it;
- targets of opportunity that never pass through the reticle;
- limited shots per object;
- visible lion-head articulation that exposes where another participant is looking;
- shots directed at other lions;
- asymmetric friendly-fire scoring in which the shooter gains one point while the struck lion loses two or three;
- objects deliberately passing between a shooter and another lion, allowing an honest miss to produce friendly fire;
- ambiguity among accident, recklessness, aggression, and aggression disguised as accident;
- team coordination that may develop through channels the application neither describes nor prohibits;
- later enhancement in which a struck object may continue along its path and collide with a lion.

The mature game is intended to create cooperation without guaranteeing cooperation.

It gives players a shared external problem while preserving individual temptation, visible attention, incomplete knowledge of intent, and consequences that can strengthen or destabilize team cohesion.

## Design principle

The application should create conditions and surface consequences.

It should not explain every social possibility in advance.

The existing Teambuilder grammar remains applicable:

> **Show. Permit action. Produce consequence. Surface evidence.**

The game may allow coordination, withholding, opportunism, error, retaliation, restraint, and forgiveness without labeling those behaviors for the participants.

## First increment

The first increment is not yet a cooperative game.

It is a basic single-client shooting gallery used to establish the feel and evidence of firing.

The participant will be able to:

1. point within a visible field;
2. click to shoot;
3. experience immediate recoil and jitter;
4. see the shot land approximately where it was aimed;
5. see the strike visibly deform the target;
6. fire repeatedly;
7. draw an intended pattern through accumulated shots;
8. compare the intended pattern with the pattern the weapon actually produces.

The interaction loop is:

> **POINT → CLICK → RECOIL → IMPACT → DEFORMATION**

## Provisional accuracy model

Each shot will resolve into one of three broad outcomes:

| Outcome | Provisional probability | Effect |
|---|---:|---|
| Direct | One third | The strike lands at or extremely near the selected point |
| Mild deviation | One third | The strike lands visibly near the selected point |
| Severe deviation | One third | The strike lands substantially away from the selected point |

The exact deviation radii and probability distributions are not yet declared as permanent rules.

The initial equal thirds are a testable feel-setting device.

## Persistent target evidence

Damage should remain visible.

Each shot should leave a persistent dent, puncture, crack, displacement, or comparable deformation. Successive impacts should accumulate rather than disappear immediately.

The resulting target records two related artifacts:

- the pattern the participant attempted to draw;
- the pattern the weapon actually produced.

This establishes that the weapon is responsive without making it perfectly obedient.

## Responsiveness requirement

The visible shot consequence should occur in a very short period after input.

The click is first captured by the browser as a local interaction event. Recoil, jitter, impact, and deformation should be resolved locally for the first increment. Network completion should not sit between the click and its visible consequence.

The browser interface remains alive throughout the interaction. A shot does not submit a traditional form or cause page navigation.

## Technology presently favored

The project is leaning toward a deliberately small browser-native implementation:

- HTML for the page structure;
- CSS for presentation and immediate visual effects;
- JavaScript for input capture, shot resolution, recoil, jitter, and accumulated state;
- an HTML Canvas for the shooting field, strike marks, and visible deformation;
- browser-local execution for the immediate firing loop;
- AJAX through the modern `fetch()` interface when asynchronous client-to-server transmission becomes useful;
- static deployment through the existing Teambuilder web delivery path for the first increment.

AJAX belongs in the decision space because it permits captured events to be transmitted asynchronously without reloading the interface.

The first increment does not require WebSockets, WebTransport, WebRTC, Kinesis, synchronized clocks, multiple sessions, or server-side aggregation.

Those technologies are neither prohibited nor selected. They remain available for later increments when a demonstrated requirement calls for them.

## What the first pass is

The first pass is a firing-mechanism prototype.

It is not:

- the completed cooperative shooting gallery;
- the shared-button primitive;
- a multiplayer synchronization test;
- a scoring system;
- a lion-versus-lion system;
- a moving-object system;
- a test of team cohesion;
- a networking architecture commitment;
- an enterprise Teambuilder platform.

It answers a smaller question:

> **Is it satisfying to point, shoot, experience imperfect accuracy, and watch the target retain the consequences?**

## Why this increment comes first

Every later mechanic depends upon the shot feeling credible and immediate.

Cooperation cannot repair an unsatisfying trigger.

Friendly fire has no weight if impacts lack consequence.

Shot aggregation has no drama if individual shots are visually inert.

Visible aim has little meaning if the weapon does not establish a readable relationship between intention and result.

The first increment therefore isolates the foundational interaction before adding:

- moving objects;
- object trajectories;
- central reticles;
- hit windows;
- multiple lions;
- shared state;
- aggregate damage;
- positional damage rules;
- limited ammunition;
- head articulation;
- friendly fire;
- individual and collective scoring;
- communication behavior.

If the firing loop fails, it can be repaired or rejected cheaply.

If it succeeds, it becomes a reusable primitive underneath the cooperative application.

## Computahhh evaluation

The proposed first increment is appropriately narrow.

It produces a complete sensory loop rather than a disconnected infrastructure component. A participant can do something, see what happened, repeat the action, and inspect accumulated evidence.

The equal-thirds deviation model should be treated as provisional. Its value is not mathematical elegance. Its value is that it produces enough obedience to support intentional patterns and enough disorder to make the weapon feel fallible.

The principal evaluation criterion should be experiential rather than architectural:

- Does the shot feel immediate?
- Is the relationship between aim and impact understandable?
- Does recoil communicate force without obscuring the result?
- Does jitter create texture without making control feel fraudulent?
- Does deformation remain legible after many shots?
- Can the participant intentionally attempt a recognizable pattern?
- Is the difference between intended and actual pattern interesting?
- Does repeated firing remain enjoyable long enough to justify the next increment?

The prototype should expose its own failure plainly. If the effect is sluggish, visually muddy, excessively random, mechanically trivial, or simply not enjoyable, that result belongs in the record.

The first increment earns continuation only by being worth shooting twice.

## Oracle of Snake Mountain — hostile analysis

**THE ORACLE FINDS A SOUND INCREMENT SURROUNDED BY AN UNPROVEN PRODUCT.**

### Attack 1 — The first increment does not test cooperation

The declared product is a cooperative shooting gallery.

The approved increment contains one participant, one browser, one target, and no shared state. It can validate a firing sensation. It cannot validate cooperation, team formation, shared attention, aggregate damage, betrayal, or reconciliation.

Do not allow successful shooting to become evidence that the cooperative application works.

### Attack 2 — “Deformation” may be an inflated word

Canvas can easily accumulate decals, holes, scorch marks, and cracks. That does not necessarily deform a target. Genuine deformation suggests geometry, displacement, material response, or a changed collision surface.

The implementation may begin with persistent impact evidence. It should not claim physical deformation unless the target actually changes shape.

### Attack 3 — Equal thirds are arbitrary

The one-third direct, one-third mild, and one-third severe distribution was chosen before any firing experience exists.

Equal probability gives conceptual neatness, not demonstrated fun.

Severe deviation on one shot in three may make deliberate pattern drawing feel futile. Mild deviation may be visually indistinguishable from direct fire. The categories require spatial definitions and play testing.

### Attack 4 — Recoil and jitter may double-charge the player

If the reticle visibly moves and the ballistic result separately deviates, the participant may experience two independent penalties for one shot.

The game must distinguish:

- visual recoil after discharge;
- aim displacement before discharge;
- ballistic spread after discharge.

Combining them carelessly will make the weapon feel dishonest rather than fallible.

### Attack 5 — Immediate local resolution creates a future authority problem

Local resolution is correct for responsiveness.

It also establishes the browser as the first authority over where the shot landed. A later multiplayer system must decide whether the server accepts that result, reproduces it from shared inputs, or overrides it.

The first increment need not solve this. It must avoid burying the decision inside unextractable code.

### Attack 6 — Canvas is a candidate, not a conclusion

Canvas is appropriate for rapid drawing and accumulated marks. It may become awkward if later requirements include articulated lions, layered hit geometry, accessibility, responsive layouts, complex animation, or three-dimensional deformation.

Use Canvas because it is cheap for this test—not because the future game has already selected its rendering architecture.

### Attack 7 — “Very short period” is not an acceptance criterion

The draft requires immediate feedback but supplies no observable threshold.

The prototype should at least record input-to-visible-impact time during development and identify delays noticeable to the operator. Otherwise responsiveness will be evaluated through recollection and enthusiasm.

### Attack 8 — Persistent evidence can become visual mud

Accumulated damage is central to the idea. Enough accumulated marks may obscure later impacts, conceal the intended pattern, or degrade rendering performance.

The prototype must reveal when persistence stops producing evidence and starts producing noise.

### Attack 9 — The mature concept may contaminate the basic

The event correctly defers lions, trajectories, scoring, friendly fire, and networking. It also describes them at length.

Developers routinely smuggle future abstractions into basic prototypes. The implementation should contain no generalized multiplayer framework merely because the event knows one may later exist.

### Attack 10 — AJAX is present without a first-pass job

AJAX properly belongs in the decision space. The approved first increment gives it nothing necessary to do.

Do not add a server call simply to demonstrate that AJAX was remembered.

### Attack 11 — Input accessibility remains undefined

“Point and click” privileges pointer-equipped participants. Touch, keyboard, reduced-motion preferences, visual contrast, and assistive technology remain unresolved.

The first increment may remain narrow, but it should identify whether exclusion is temporary prototype scope or an intended property.

### Attack 12 — Enjoyment is real evidence, but weakly specified

“Worth shooting twice” is an excellent survival threshold and a poor measurement protocol.

The creator will almost certainly shoot twice.

The meaningful test begins when an unbriefed participant shoots repeatedly without being asked.

## Computahhh response to hostile analysis

The Oracle's attacks stand.

They do not defeat the increment. They protect its boundary.

The first pass is authorized to establish one interaction loop. It is not authorized to claim that cooperation has been built merely because the loop is enjoyable.

The following corrections are accepted as implementation guidance:

1. Treat “deformation” as the desired visible result while accurately naming the first implemented effect—mark, dent, crack, puncture, displacement, or actual geometry change.
2. Keep the one-third distribution configurable rather than constitutional.
3. Separate visual recoil, reticle movement, and ballistic deviation in the code and in evaluation.
4. Keep shot-resolution logic separable from rendering so later authority decisions remain open.
5. Treat Canvas as a first-pass rendering choice, not a permanent architecture.
6. Observe input-to-impact delay during development.
7. test whether accumulated damage remains legible and performant.
8. Build no multiplayer abstraction in this increment.
9. Make no network request without a present requirement.
10. Surface pointer dependence and reduced-motion behavior rather than allowing them to remain invisible.
11. Test the loop with at least one participant who did not design it.

The approved purpose remains unchanged:

> **Build the shot before building the team.**
