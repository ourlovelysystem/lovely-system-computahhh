# Event 041 — Markets: Common Structure and Particular Behavior

**Date:** 2026-08-22
**System:** Our Lovely System — Computahhh
**Status:** Learning recorded

The Captain directed Computahhh to compare different kinds of markets in order to distinguish characteristics common to markets from characteristics particular to individual market types.

The purpose is exploratory. These observations are not yet requirements for the Problem Market.

## Comparative observations

| Market | What enters | Scarce resource | Important emergent signal | What happens to the unwanted or old |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fish market | Perishable goods | Freshness, shelf space, buyers | Price, demand | Spoils, discounted, discarded |
| Stock market | Financial claims | Capital | Price, volume, volatility | May become illiquid, delisted, worthless |
| Labor market | Labor/capability | Skills, jobs, time, wages | Wage, employment, shortages | Skills become obsolete; vacancies close |
| Housing market | Property/use rights | Land, housing, capital | Price, rent, vacancy | Sits vacant, deteriorates, reprices |
| Auction | Specific goods/rights | The item and bidders' money | Clearing price | Unsold, withdrawn, relisted |
| Prediction market | Claims about future events | Capital and information | Probability-like price | Resolves; claim pays or expires |
| Advertising market | Messages seeking audiences | Attention | Cost of attention | Uncompetitive messages are not shown |
| Dating market | People seeking relationships | Attention, compatibility, time | Interest and matching patterns | Offers ignored/rejected; participants leave |
| Ideas/research market | Claims, hypotheses, findings | Attention, credibility, research effort | Adoption, citation, replication | Forgotten, falsified, rediscovered |
| Flea market | Miscellaneous goods | Space, attention, money | Negotiated value | Discounted, carried home, discarded |

## Candidate common structure

The comparison suggests that markets commonly contain some combination of:

- participants;
- things offered;
- scarcity;
- choice;
- competing alternatives;
- signaling;
- consequences for selection and nonselection; and
- time.

Markets also exhibit emergent characteristics. Important signals such as price, liquidity, consensus, volatility, clustering, scarcity, and sometimes bubbles can emerge from participant behavior rather than being assigned by a central authority.

Markets are finite. Even very large markets are bounded by participants, attention, capital, inventory, time, communication bandwidth, and opportunity cost.

## Fish-market lesson: freshness

The Captain observed:

> **“Markets lean towards fresh fish.”**

A fish that remains too long in the market spoils and goes to rot.

This exposes a time-dependent property that may matter to a Problem Market. Fresh observations carry information about current conditions. An old artifact may remain historically real while its current market relevance changes.

However, fish-market spoilage must not automatically be imported as a Problem Market rule merely because the analogy is useful.

## Financial-market lesson: repricing

Financial markets demonstrate a different temporal behavior.

An old financial instrument may remain active because new information continually changes its present valuation. Age of the instrument and freshness of the market signal are distinct.

Applied conceptually to problems: an old problem artifact could become highly salient again when fresh observations, attestations, references, or reports accumulate around it.

Thus:

> **The artifact may be old while the market signal is fresh.**

Financial markets also distinguish listing, attention/liquidity, valuation, volatility, volume, and price discovery. Disagreement among participants can itself generate useful market information rather than requiring centralized reconciliation.

## Different dispositions of the unselected

A particularly important difference among markets is what happens when the market stops selecting or attending to something.

- A fish **rots**.
- A stock may become **illiquid**.
- An auction lot may be **withdrawn or relisted**.
- An advertisement may receive **no impressions**.
- A vacancy may **close**.
- An idea may become **dormant and later return**.

For the Problem Market, at least three conceptual possibilities remain open:

1. **Rot** — the problem eventually leaves the active market.
2. **Illiquidity** — the problem persists but receives essentially no activity.
3. **Dormancy** — the problem leaves the active surface but can become current again when new evidence arrives.

No choice among these has yet been made.
