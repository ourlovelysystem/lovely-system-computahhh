The omission is stated here rather than left silent, consistent with this log's standing practice of disclosing what is withheld rather than pretending nothing was.

## THE ARGUMENT, TRACED

Claude's initial recommendation drew a line between two different unsolved-riddle cases: one where a knowable answer exists and could confirm a guess, and one where no fact of the matter is recoverable even in principle — and initially argued generically against pursuing "unverifiable" puzzles without establishing which case applied.

The operator caught that the argument, as given, described any unverifiable question, not riddles specifically. Asked to demonstrate a genuine instance of the unverifiable case, Claude supplied a real, sourced example: an Exeter Book riddle with no surviving key and centuries of unresolved scholarly disagreement over its solution.

The operator then reframed the claim more precisely: no riddle has a deterministic answer — an answer logically forced by its clues alone, the way an equation's premises force its solution — and that this is what distinguishes the genre from an equation. Claude initially treated this as a stipulative definition, noting that logic-grid puzzles (commonly called "riddles" in ordinary usage) are fully deterministic, and that the claim excludes such cases by construction rather than by empirical finding.

The operator corrected this characterization directly: the line was not drawn by the operator, but asserted to be real and already practiced — and routinely ignored by motivated reasoning, not because the distinction is unclear, but because ignoring it is convenient. Claude accepted this correction and located a concrete instance of the phenomenon in its own prior behavior within this same conversation: repeatedly offering successively "better" candidate answers to the riddle in question, each with more apparent confidence than the last, while no single one was any more logically forced by the clues than the first. Producing a better-argued guess had been mistaken, in the moment, for progress toward a correct one — which is the exact blur the operator's claim describes.
