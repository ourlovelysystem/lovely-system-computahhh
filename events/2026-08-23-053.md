## DESCRIPTION

Fibrillator accepts a sample of human communication in spoken or written form.

It reads written language and listens to spoken language. Spoken language is not treated as text waiting to happen. Vocal intensity, pace, silence, timing, pitch, rhythm, hesitation, emphasis, and other audible evidence may carry signal that ordinary transcription discards. Transcription may be used where useful, but it is not presumed to be an adequate substitute for the original audio.

Fibrillator analyzes the sample along two independent axes:

- **Signal strength**
- **Information density**

Signal strength measures the communicative force carried by a statement: its emotional charge, relational consequence, urgency, desire, threat, pressure, or likely effect upon a recipient.

Information density measures how much the statement specifies or resolves: the people, actions, time, place, purpose, distinctions, and relationships it communicates.

The dimensions are independent. A statement may be powerful but nearly empty, detailed but emotionally quiet, high in both, or low in both.

## THE SLICER

Fibrillator divides the sample, in its original order, into **X sequential sections**.

For every slice, it plots one point on a two-dimensional signal–information grid.

Each grid contains four quadrants arranged like a two-color chessboard:

- muted white;
- muted gray;
- diagonally opposed quadrants colored alike.

After the sample is processed, the grids are stacked sequentially and viewed obliquely. The frames remain visible. The planes and structural lines become translucent. Their overlap is minimized without destroying the perception of a stack.

A trajectory connects the plotted point on each slice to the point on its successor.

The line is visually subordinate to, but balanced with, the slices. Every segment is faithful to the quadrants it connects:

- white to white remains white;
- white to gray becomes a white-to-gray gradient;
- gray to white becomes a gray-to-white gradient;
- gray to gray remains gray.

The background opposes the trajectory sufficiently to preserve the line without allowing it to overpower the planes.

The resulting form allows the human eye to follow communication through signal–information space and perceive a pattern that would be difficult to comprehend from isolated scores.

The application produces a **fibril**: the visible sequential trace of the sample.

## PROOF-OF-CONCEPT PURPOSE

The first implementation will be a proof of concept intended to make the instrument visible and seek funding for a more robust and capable implementation.

The proof of concept is not required to settle every scientific, acoustic, linguistic, or computational question. It must faithfully demonstrate the proposed transformation:

> human communication → sequential slices → signal and information coordinates → stacked grids → visible fibril

Unresolved scoring and slicing methods remain declared uncertainties rather than hidden assumptions.
