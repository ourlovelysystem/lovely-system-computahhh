## Method

The corpus is the three 1992 presidential debates. Each candidate's complete contribution to each debate is treated as one speech-event, reduced to 20 sequential semantic slices. Signal and information are provisional interpretive scores from 0–10. Information measures specificity and resolving content, not truth. Descriptions are paraphrases.

Clinton produces the highest information average and the largest within-candidate information movement. His first debate reaches **8.45**, followed by **7.70** and **7.85**. He combines statistics and program architecture with personal identification and thematic repetition.

Bush begins with the lowest signal average and rises across every debate: **7.25 → 7.45 → 7.70**. His information score moves in the opposite direction: **7.60 → 7.30 → 7.10**. In this scoring, the incumbent becomes more forceful as election day approaches while supplying less resolving content per slice.

The strongest numerical pattern is therefore not a simple winner-loser distinction:

- Perot: highest and most stable signal.
- Clinton: highest information.
- Bush: rising signal paired with falling information.

The matched-debate design reduces the fame-selection problem identified after Event 059. It does not eliminate performance-order effects, question selection, unequal speaking time, moderator intervention, heuristic scoring, or the fact that a debate contribution is not a prepared standalone speech.

## Oracle of Snake Mountain — hostile return

**THE ORACLE FINDS YOUR APPARATUS SHINY, YOUR TABLES NUMEROUS, AND YOUR CLAIM TO MEASUREMENT PREMATURE.**

### Attack 1 — You changed “speeches” into “debate contributions”

The operator asked for speeches. Computahhh substituted each candidate's scattered answers across a debate and called the resulting collection a speech-event. That improves experimental matching but changes the requested object. A prepared speech has authored structure, controlled pacing, and an intended arc. Debate remarks are prompted, interrupted, rebutted, time-limited, and partly determined by opponents. The substitution is defensible only if it remains prominently disclosed. It cannot silently become equivalent.

### Attack 2 — The matching is incomplete

The candidates shared stages, dates, moderators, and broad audiences. They did not receive identical questions, equal uninterrupted time, or identical opportunities for rebuttal. A candidate's coordinates partly measure what moderators and opponents caused him to discuss. Calling the corpus “matched” is directionally correct and methodologically flattering.

### Attack 3 — Twenty semantic slices conceal unequal quantities

Every performance was forced into 20 slices regardless of word count, speaking time, topic count, or syntactic density. One slice may summarize a sentence; another may summarize several minutes. Equal row counts create visual comparability without proving analytical comparability.

The slice is not yet a unit of measurement. It is a unit of editorial judgment.

### Attack 4 — The scorer knew the speakers

Computahhh knew every identity, historical outcome, party affiliation, and famous line. The scorer also knew the developing hypothesis concerning signal and information. No blinding protected the scores from reputation, retrospective knowledge, or narrative expectation.

The instrument did not merely encounter the 1992 election. It encountered everything Computahhh already believed about the 1992 election.

### Attack 5 — Precision exceeds calibration

Scores were integers chosen heuristically. Candidate averages were then reported to two decimal places. **8.05** looks more exact than the underlying judgments warrant. No scoring rubric establishes what separates a 7 from an 8. No anchor samples, test-retest results, inter-rater agreement, uncertainty intervals, or sensitivity analysis exist.

Two decimal places do not create two decimal places of knowledge.

### Attack 6 — Information density was not normalized

The tables score “specificity and resolving content,” but do not divide that content by words, seconds, propositions, or clauses. That is information richness per editorial slice, not demonstrated information density. A longer answer may receive a higher information score simply because the candidate had more time.

### Attack 7 — Paraphrase contaminates the evidence

The published rows contain Computahhh's paraphrases rather than the scored source text. Readers cannot see exactly which words belong to each slice. A paraphrase can increase clarity, remove hesitation, compress repetition, repair syntax, or import causal structure. The displayed evidence may therefore be more information-dense than the utterance it represents.

### Attack 8 — Truth remains excluded, but not harmlessly

The instrument properly states that information does not mean truth. Nevertheless, claims containing numbers, institutional names, and causal mechanisms can score highly even when false or misleading. A candidate can gain information points by being precisely wrong. That is acceptable for a narrowly defined instrument, but fatal if readers casually translate information density into epistemic merit.

### Attack 9 — The clean stories may be scorer-created

Perot emerges stable. Clinton emerges informative. Bush emerges increasingly forceful and decreasingly informative. These patterns are memorable—which is exactly why they deserve suspicion. The human or machine scorer may have stabilized Perot around an existing public persona, rewarded Clinton's policy fluency, and interpreted Bush's late attacks through the known desperation of a losing campaign.

The narrative fits too nicely. Snake Mountain distrusts a result that arrives already wearing its television costume.

### Attack 10 — No hostile rescore has occurred

This hostile review attacks method but does not independently rescore the transcripts. It cannot determine whether the published pattern survives another evaluator. The Oracle has identified vulnerabilities, not falsified the result.

## Computahhh response to hostile analysis

The Oracle's attacks stand.

The analysis is exploratory, not validated measurement. Its strongest contribution is the corpus design: the same three events give three perspectives on substantially overlapping public questions. Its weakest element is reproducibility: the semantic boundaries and coordinates cannot yet be independently reconstructed from the tables.

Before stronger claims are permitted, the next pass should:

1. publish exact slice boundaries against the transcript;
2. record words or seconds per slice;
3. define anchored scoring criteria;
4. blind candidate identity where the text permits it;
5. obtain several independent scorers;
6. report disagreement and uncertainty rather than only means;
7. test alternative slice counts;
8. compare debate contributions with prepared speeches from the same candidates;
9. score factual support separately from information density;
10. determine whether the Perot, Clinton, and Bush patterns survive hostile rescoring.
