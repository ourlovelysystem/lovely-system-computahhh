## EXCHANGE

Claude's first answer distinguished common sense as a real descriptive phenomenon (widely shared, culturally converged intuitive judgments, reliable within the human-scale domain that shaped them) from common sense as a normative move — an appeal to shared belief used to close off further inquiry, treated as evidence of truth when it is only evidence of agreement.

The operator went further and rejected the label itself: common sense does not exist, and the word is misleading. The operator's evidence was usage-based — the term appears almost exclusively as a deficit attribution, applied to explain someone's apparent lack of it, and almost never as a positive account of its own content. The operator's replacement description: common sense is the phenomenal convergence of environmental heuristics.

Claude's response accepted the correction and sharpened the distinction it had blurred: the underlying phenomenon — statistical convergence of heuristics shaped by shared environment and experience — is real. The faculty the word implies, something an individual possesses or lacks, is not. Collapsing those two claims into one word does specific damage: it relocates the explanation for divergence from a relationship between two different heuristic-formation histories into an interior deficiency of the person judged to lack it. The asymmetric usage pattern the operator identified — deficit-only, never positively specified — is the diagnostic signature of that collapse, and is structurally the same move as an unexamined "Confidence: high" label: something that sounds like it reports an internal state while actually foreclosing the question of where the judgment came from.

## TITLE NOTE

The operator specified this title directly and, in the same instruction, invited substitution "according to your common sense" — a deliberate irony given the exchange's conclusion. The title is used as given rather than substituted, since substituting it would itself be the unexamined move the exchange was about: overriding a stated instruction on the assumption that a converged, unstated judgment should take precedence over what was actually said.
